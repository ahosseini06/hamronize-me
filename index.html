Live channel vocoder · runs in Chrome

Your voice, playing a synth.

Sing or talk into a microphone while you hold chords on a MIDI keyboard. The vocoder splits your voice into up to 32 frequency bands and uses the shape of each one to carve the synth underneath it. The synth ends up speaking with your mouth.

It is built for playing live, so every sample of audio processing happens on the browser's audio render thread. The main thread never touches a sample — it only draws the picture.

What's in it

The filterbank

12 to 32 log-spaced bands, each a pair of cascaded bandpass filters. Fractional formant shifting, spectral tilt, and a freeze that holds the current shape of your voice indefinitely while you play under it.

Consonants you can hear

A voiced/unvoiced detector watches the energy balance in your voice and swaps noise into the high bands on consonants. It is the difference between a vocoder you can understand and a buzz.

The carrier

An eight-voice synth with up to seven anti-aliased oscillators per voice plus a sub. Poly, mono or legato, with glide, velocity, pitch bend and vibrato. Stereo width comes from spreading detuned voices.

Tempo-locked movement

Two LFOs synced to one tempo by rhythmic division, routable to any of seventeen destinations. A slow sweep over formant is where the classic talking-vocoder movement lives.

Two separate spaces

Your dry voice and the vocoded signal each get their own tempo-synced echo and reverb. Keep the voice dry and in front while the synthetic part blooms behind it.

Built to be played

Right-click any control to map it to a MIDI CC. Twelve presets, a panic button on Escape, and a keyboard you can play from the computer keys if you have no hardware.

Latency, honestly

The goal was under 10 milliseconds. Here is what actually happens, and why:

PathRealistic
MIDI in → audio out~6–10 msone render quantum plus the output buffer
Mic in → audio out~15–25 msbounded by Chrome's capture stack

The synth path genuinely hits the target. The microphone round trip does not, in any browser, and no amount of application code changes that — the floor is set by the browser's audio input path and the operating system's driver.

So the instrument measures instead of claiming. It asks for the smallest buffer the browser will grant, disables every scrap of echo cancellation and noise suppression on the way in, and keeps the audio graph to three nodes. Then it reports the real figures back to you — click the RT readout for a breakdown, and it will tell you plainly when the browser has given it a number it does not trust.

What you need

  • Chrome or Edge. It needs AudioWorklet, Web MIDI and cross-origin isolation. Safari and Firefox have no Web MIDI.
  • Headphones. Not optional — open speakers feed the vocoder straight back into itself.
  • A microphone. Anything works. A dynamic mic close to your mouth works best.
  • A MIDI keyboard, ideally. Without one the on-screen keyboard plays from Z–M and Q–P.

Your microphone audio never leaves your machine. There is no server, no account and no analytics — the page loads and everything after that happens locally.

Getting a sound you like

Intelligibility comes mostly from three controls. Raise SIBILANCE until consonants cut through. Keep the vocoder's ATTACK fast, one to three milliseconds, so plosives stay sharp. And give the carrier something to work with — DRIVE, UNISON and SUB all add harmonics.

That last one matters more than it sounds: a vocoder can only reshape energy that is already there, so a thin carrier vocodes into near-silence. Set the GATE just above your room noise and the filterbank will stay shut between phrases.

How it's built

Vanilla JavaScript, no dependencies, no build step. The DSP runs in an AudioWorklet and allocates nothing after startup, because a garbage collection pause on the audio thread is an audible dropout. Controls, MIDI and metering move between threads through a SharedArrayBuffer rather than messages, so nothing on the main thread can block the audio.

It ships with 166 tests. The worklet has no imports and touches only three globals, which means the whole signal chain can be loaded in plain Node and checked numerically — filter selectivity, alias rejection, limiter ceilings, echo timing against the beat. The rest drive real Chrome with a synthetic microphone.
